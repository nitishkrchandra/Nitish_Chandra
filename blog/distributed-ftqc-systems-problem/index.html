Distributed fault-tolerant quantum computing is a systems problem

Why error correction, optical interconnects, and logical execution have to be designed as one system.

Quantum computers will need many reliable qubits to run useful fault-tolerant algorithms. One possible route is to connect smaller quantum processors rather than make every qubit, control line and in­ter­ac­tion fit inside a single device. A network of quantum processing units (QPUs) could be more flexible: processors can specialize, hardware may be manufactured in smaller modules, and en­tan­gle­ment can enable in­ter­ac­tions between distant qubits.

But there is a large gap between connecting quantum processors and computing reliably across them. That gap is where much of my research sits. Dis­trib­uted fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) is not solely a networking problem, nor solely an error-correction problem. It is a systems problem in which choices at one layer change the per­for­mance and re­li­a­bil­i­ty of the others. The general landscape and ar­chi­tec­tural motivations are reviewed in the dis­trib­uted-quantum-computing survey by Caleffi and collaborators [1].

1. What does it mean to distribute a quantum computer?

There are at least two distinct things we might distribute: physical qubits belonging to one encoded logical qubit, or complete encoded logical qubits that interact across processors. They require different kinds of com­mu­ni­ca­tion. In the first case, the in­ter­con­nect may par­tic­i­pate in every error-correction cycle. In the second, it can be reserved for operations between encoded blocks, allowing much of the routine correction to remain local.

To make the dis­tinc­tion concrete, suppose a stabilizer code uses many physical qubits to store protected logical in­for­ma­tion. Maintaining that in­for­ma­tion means repeatedly measuring stabilizers and decoding their mea­sure­ment outcomes. If the code spans QPUs, some of those checks may require remote en­tan­gle­ment during every error-correction cycle. If instead each QPU holds a complete code block, its error correction may be mostly local, but logical operations between blocks require fault-tolerant interfaces.

Our work on ar­chi­tec­tural approaches to dis­trib­uted FTQC formalizes three broad patterns [2]:

  1. Small cooperating nodes: shared multipartite entangled resources support nonlocal stabilizer measurements.
  2. One partitioned code block: most checks stay local, but checks across partition boundaries use remote operations.
  3. Separate encoded blocks: each QPU hosts one or more code blocks, while logical gates use appropriate inter-module protocols.

These ar­chi­tec­tures cannot be compared solely by counting their processors. One ar­chi­tec­ture spends en­tan­gle­ment on repeated syndrome extraction; another spends it largely on logical in­ter­ac­tions. Their com­mu­ni­ca­tion demand depends on code distance, circuit depth and the workload's pattern of logical gates.

2. How can a remote quantum interaction be implemented?

A common building block is a Bell pair shared between two modules. The network generates en­tan­gle­ment between com­mu­ni­ca­tion qubits, and local gates, mea­sure­ments and classical feed­for­ward use that resource to implement a remote operation on com­pu­ta­tion­al qubits. This is the principle behind quantum gate tele­por­ta­tion.

The experimental dis­tinc­tion matters. In 2025, Main and collaborators demonstrated deterministic gate tele­por­ta­tion con­di­tion­al on having successfully generated the shared en­tan­gle­ment between two trapped-ion modules, and used repeated remote gates within dis­trib­uted circuits [3]. In September 2026, Ainley and collaborators reported remote syndrome mea­sure­ments and error correction across two trapped-ion processors, including experiments involving a dis­trib­uted repetition code and feed­for­ward [4].

These demonstrations answer important physical questions. They do not, by themselves, tell us the logical error rate or resource overhead of a large encoded com­pu­ta­tion. At that scale, Bell pairs must arrive often enough to support the many checks and logical gates a fault-tolerant com­pu­ta­tion needs.

3. Why a network is not simply a long wire

On a classical network, retrying a lost packet mainly costs time and bandwidth. For a quantum computer, a failed en­tan­gle­ment attempt can also cause the stored com­pu­ta­tion­al state to wait while its physical qubits continue to accumulate errors.

For a deliberately simplified example, suppose in­de­pen­dent en­tan­gle­ment attempts succeed with prob­a­bil­i­ty q. If we keep retrying until the first success, then the number N of attempts has a geometric dis­tri­bu­tion:

At a fixed attempt duration ta, the mean attempt time is ta/q before accounting for reset, cooling, routing and feed­for­ward. If com­mu­ni­ca­tion ports are shared, contention can make the actual delay longer and dependent on the schedule. Meanwhile, an encoded data block may need to keep correcting errors during that wait.

This is why I find it misleading to evaluate a link using fidelity alone. A useful in­ter­con­nect is char­ac­ter­ized by the fidelity and rate of usable en­tan­gle­ment, the waiting-time dis­tri­bu­tion, available com­mu­ni­ca­tion qubits and the errors induced by the remote protocol.

4. A computation passes through several layers

In an end-to-end ar­chi­tec­ture, the in­for­ma­tion must pass through a linked chain of decisions:

01

Code and logical operationsWhat is encoded, and which protected in­struc­tions must be executed?

02

Physical placementWhere are the data qubits, ancillas and com­mu­ni­ca­tion qubits?

03

Remote protocolsHow are nonlocal checks and logical gates im­ple­ment­ed using en­tan­gle­ment?

04

Scheduling and decodingWhat waits, competes for resources and generates correlated errors?

05

Logical outcomeWhat logical failure prob­a­bil­i­ty, latency and hardware cost result?

Each layer constrains the next, but feedback matters too. A slow schedule increases memory noise, which might require a larger code. A larger code requires more checks, which increases network demand. This feedback is a reason to perform ar­chi­tec­ture-aware co-design rather than independently optimize a compiler, decoder and quantum switch.

Our optically networked trapped-ion work connects remote en­tan­gle­ment generation, spatial and temporal multiplexing, qubit and memory noise, and the prepa­ra­tion of a protected mea­sure­ment-based resource state [5]. More recently, our network-con­strained FTQC framework studies how finite generation rates, com­mu­ni­ca­tion qubits and bandwidth contention change execution time and error estimates [6]. Separate work on quantum-switch policies shows that storing, distilling and swapping Bell pairs involve their own timing and fidelity tradeoffs [7].

5. What should we optimize?

There is no single metric that captures dis­trib­uted FTQC. Minimizing remote operations may produce a poor schedule. Maximizing Bell-pair fidelity may reduce throughput. Minimizing physical-qubit count may leave too few ports for timely correction. These tradeoffs depend on the intended logical workload: a quantum memory and a com­pu­ta­tion requiring frequent remote gates can favor very different partitions and com­mu­ni­ca­tion designs.

A more informative comparison should report at least logical error per operation or cycle, completion time, com­mu­ni­ca­tion overhead and total hardware resources. Which design is preferable depends on the intended workload and target re­li­a­bil­i­ty. In particular, quantum-memory per­for­mance and the cost of universal logical com­pu­ta­tion should not be conflated.

What should a network service promise?

A useful ar­chi­tec­ture boundary is an en­tan­gle­ment-delivery service: the network supplies pairs with specified quality, resource use and completion-time statistics; the fault-tolerant in­struc­tion requests those pairs and declares how they will be used. Such a contract makes the hardware model replaceable without pretending the remote operation has zero latency.

Imagine two modules connected by a switch. Delivering one end-to-end pair might require generating two elementary links and performing swapping; another system may directly connect the modules. Their physical mechanisms differ, but both can be evaluated in terms of how often they deliver useful en­tan­gle­ment, what error cor­re­la­tions survive, and what other work must wait.

In a practical benchmark I would fix the logical task and target re­li­a­bil­i­ty, then report qubit occupation, pairs consumed, mean and tail latency, and the failure prob­a­bil­i­ty of the encoded com­pu­ta­tion. This also separates useful quantum memory from universal quantum processing: an ar­chi­tec­ture excellent at storing dis­trib­uted logical qubits may still have expensive nonlocal logical gates.

My perspective: connecting the pieces

I see an op­por­tu­ni­ty to develop a cross-layer methodology in which a logical com­pu­ta­tion is lowered into fault-tolerant primitives, mapped onto a modular ar­chi­tec­ture, scheduled against a realistic en­tan­gle­ment service, and evaluated with a noise model reflecting how those operations were actually carried out.

The longer-term goal is a machine that is not merely network connected, but demonstrably fault tolerant at the logical level. The key research question is therefore not “Can two QPUs interact?” It is “How can we arrange the complete com­pu­ta­tion so that the network enables scalability without erasing the protection provided by quantum error correction?”
